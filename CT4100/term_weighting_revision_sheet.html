CT4100 Topic Guide

Term weighting

Learn why some matching terms should count more than others.

Exam-use protocol

Before writing, identify the command word: calculate/trace requires an ordered derivation; explain requires a definition, mechanism and assumption; compare/design requires matched criteria, a justified choice and a limitation. The topic-specific recall and worked routes below are the material to adapt—not text to copy blindly.

Part-by-part recall: definitions, assumptions and mechanics

Definition: tf is within-document evidence; idf=log(N/df) discounts common terms; length normalisation removes verbosity bias. BM25: saturated tf plus idf and length correction; k1 controls saturation and b length effect. Compare: basic tf-idf is linear in tf; BM25 dampens repetition. Question cue: explain a factor's ranking effect, not just its name.

Worked practice 1 — paper-style synthesis (recurring-topic pattern; not a quoted paper prompt) weighting

Question demand: N=1000, df(t)=10; compare one versus ten occurrences. Plan/choice: interpret rarity then tf response. Worked answer: idf=log(100), so t is discriminative. Linear tf-idf gives ten times the tf contribution; BM25 increases but saturates. Mark-winning points: define N, df and length. Common trap: high tf proves relevance.

Worked practice 2 — paper-style synthesis (recurring-topic pattern; not a quoted paper prompt) length bias

Question demand: Explain why concatenating a document inflates basic tf-idf similarity and fix it. Plan/choice: trace tf/magnitude, then normalise. Worked answer: query-term counts grow despite unchanged topic, so unnormalised score grows. Cosine divides by length; BM25 length-normalises and saturates tf. Mark-winning points: causal length-bias explanation. Common trap: normalisation makes all documents equal.

Purpose and module relevance

Weighting makes a rare, informative term more influential than a common one. It supports CT4100 discussions of tf-idf, BM25, normalisation and scheme design.

What a question is really asking

Show why the formula makes ranking sense, not just its symbols. Plan: identify term frequency and collection rarity; calculate weights; explain length/saturation; state the ranking effect.

Prerequisites, vocabulary and notation

  • tf(t,d): occurrences of term t in d.
  • df(t): documents containing t; N: collection size.
  • idf(t)=log(N/df(t)): larger for rarer terms.
  • tf-idf: tf × idf. BM25 additionally saturates tf and normalises document length.

Method: derive a sensible weight

  1. Count tf in each candidate document.
  2. Count df across the collection, then calculate idf.
  3. Multiply tf by idf: a frequent but ubiquitous word is downweighted.
  4. For BM25, explain that repeated occurrences help less and very long documents are adjusted.
  5. Test whether the resulting order matches the query's information need.

Worked practice 3 — foundational calculation

Question

N=100. Term x occurs in 10 documents. In d1, tf=3; in d2, tf=1. Use tf-idf with idf=log10(N/df).

Model answer

idf=log10(10)=1. Thus d1 scores 3 and d2 scores 1 for x. d1 ranks higher for this one-term query. This is not proof it is more relevant, only the model's evidence.

Question interpretation and independent practice

Build confidence

Why is idf small for a stop word? Check: its df is near N, so log(N/df) is near zero.

Exam bridge

Propose a weighting scheme for product search and justify its design. Outline: tf, rarity, length, field boosts such as title, saturation, then a possible failure case.

High-value traps and corrections

  • Confusing df with tf.
  • Claiming high tf always means high relevance.
  • Writing BM25's name without explaining saturation and length normalisation.

Final answer checklist

  • Are N, df and tf defined?
  • Does each formula term have an interpretation?
  • Did I discuss a ranking consequence?
