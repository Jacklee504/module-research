Syndromes, Cosets and Decoding

The syndrome depends only on the error, so it identifies a chosen coset leader.

Rapid refresher

Medium/hard adapted practice

Paper family: Syndrome tables/standard arrays and decoding are Q3 in 2018/19-2024/25, with standard arrays explicit in 2023/24.

(a) Describe syndrome decoding of r using a known H and syndrome table. [8 marks]

Question demand: apply the definition/calculation with working. Quickest route: Compute s, look up e(s), subtract in Fq, then check cH^T=0.

Mark-aware answer: Because cH^T=0, the syndrome of r equals the syndrome of its error. The lookup chooses e; c=r-e is the decoded codeword. A zero syndrome selects e=0.

Trap: Do not correct a nonzero syndrome by changing an arbitrary coordinate.

(b) Construct the first two rows of a standard array for a linear code. [6 marks]

Question demand: First row is C; choose a lowest-weight unused coset leader e and add it to every codeword. Quickest route: First row is C; choose a lowest-weight unused coset leader e and add it to every codeword.

Mark-aware answer: Write the codewords in row one. Choose e not already used, preferably minimum weight, and list e+C underneath; each row is a coset with a common syndrome. Repeat until every ambient word appears.

Trap: Do not choose a new leader from a previous coset.

(c) Explain what a tie means in nearest-neighbour decoding. [4 marks]

Question demand: Identify two codewords/coset leaders at equal minimum distance and state a convention. Quickest route: Identify two codewords/coset leaders at equal minimum distance and state a convention.

Mark-aware answer: If r is equally close to two codewords, unique nearest-neighbour decoding is impossible. An answer must report the ambiguity or apply the explicitly supplied tie-breaking rule; it cannot claim a uniquely corrected word.

Trap: A syndrome table may encode a convention but does not remove mathematical ambiguity.
