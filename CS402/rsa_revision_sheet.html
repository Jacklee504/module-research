RSA

Show the key construction, modular arithmetic and the reason decryption returns the message.

Purpose

RSA is a central repeatable calculation. A full answer checks every key condition, shows the exponentiation, and states the number-theory fact that justifies the round trip.Exam use: Key validity, inverse working, square-and-multiply table, public/private labels and the appropriate correctness proof are present.

What the question asks, and plan

  1. From primes calculate n=pq and φ(n)=(p−1)(q−1).
  2. Choose/check e with gcd(e,φ(n))=1.
  3. Find d≡e⁻¹ mod φ(n); state public (n,e), private d.
  4. Encrypt c=mᵉ mod n, decrypt m=cᵈ mod n, using repeated squaring.
  5. Explain correctness with ed=1+kφ(n) and Euler's theorem.
  6. Question interpretation: For generate, show the gcd and inverse; for encrypt/decrypt, show modular powers; for prove correct, handle both primes rather than quietly assuming gcd(m,n)=1.

Terms and formulas

  • Modulus: n=pq, product of distinct secret primes.
  • Euler totient: φ(pq)=(p−1)(q−1) for distinct primes.
  • Public/private exponents: ed≡1 mod φ(n).
  • Euler: if gcd(m,n)=1, then m^φ(n)≡1 mod n.
  • Security intuition: recovering d from the public key is tied to factoring n; tiny classroom keys are deliberately insecure.
  • Rapid recall: n=pq, lambda(n)=lcm(p-1,q-1) or phi(n)=(p-1)(q-1), gcd(e,lambda)=1, and ed=1 mod lambda; CRT gives correctness even when m shares a factor with n.

Derivation and method

Since ed=1+kφ(n), decrypting gives (mᵉ)ᵈ=m^(1+kφ(n))=m(m^φ(n))ᵏ≡m mod n when coprime. In an exam, find inverses with the extended Euclidean algorithm and calculate powers by squaring; do not attempt a huge exponent directly. State any required message/coprimality assumptions.Exam workflow: Use extended Euclid for d, repeated squaring for powers, and a mod-p/mod-q argument followed by the Chinese remainder theorem for a complete correctness proof.

Fully worked simple example

Take p=3,q=11. Then n=33, φ=20. Choose e=3; its inverse mod 20 is d=7. Encrypt m=4: c=4³=64≡31 mod 33. Decrypt: 31²≡4, so 31⁴≡16 and 31⁷=31⁴·31²·31≡16·4·31≡4 mod 33. Public key is (33,3); private exponent is 7.

Medium/hard paper-style synthesis

Take p=5,q=11,e=3. Then n=55, phi=40, and 1=3*27-2*40, so d=27. Encrypt m=12: 12^2=34, 12^4=1 mod 55, hence c=12^3=23. Decrypt 23^27: 23^2=34, 23^4=1, and 27=16+8+2+1, so 23^27=1*1*34*23=782=12 mod 55. Public key (55,3); private exponent 27.

Two graded prompts

1. Foundation — 7 marks

For p=5,q=11,e=3, find n,φ(n),d. Exam bridge: values (3), coprimality (1), inverse working (3). Check: n=55,φ=40,d=27.

2. Exam bridge — 8 marks

Explain why an RSA public key may be published but d must not be. Check: distinguish public encryption parameters from the factorisation/private inverse; do not claim RSA is secure merely because exponentiation is hard.

Hard paper-style synthesis

Paper-style synthesis: prove RSA correctness for every residue m mod pq. Check modulo p: if p divides m both sides are 0; otherwise Fermat and ed=1 mod p-1 give m^{ed}=m. Repeat modulo q, then CRT implies equality mod pq. This closes the gap in the coprime-only Euler proof.

Common mistakes

  • Using n instead of φ(n) when finding d.
  • Choosing e not coprime to φ(n).
  • Swapping public/private components or forgetting modulus reductions.
  • Presenting the classroom example as realistic secure RSA.
  • Recovery rule: If the message is not coprime to n, switch to the prime-by-prime proof; do not cite Euler's theorem outside its hypothesis.

Checklist

  • p, q, n and φ all shown?
  • gcd and inverse checked?
  • Fast exponentiation working visible?
  • Correctness/security claim qualified?
