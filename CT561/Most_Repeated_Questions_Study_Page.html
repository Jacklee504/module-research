2. Euler's Method, Assumptions and Error 8/8 papers

Past-paper question: "Describe Euler's method of integration, and state the underlying assumptions. Under what conditions will Euler's method overestimate the true value of a stock? Support your answer by considering the numerical integration of the net flow over the time interval 0-2.5 from the model in part (a), assuming DT=0.5." 2025/26 Q1(b) [5]
Open workings

S(t + DT) = S(t) + Net Flow(t) x DT. Forward Euler replaces each area under a flow curve with a left-hand rectangle.

  • Net flow is treated as constant within each DT.
  • The stock therefore changes linearly within that step.
  • DT must be small enough for the approximation to be acceptable.

From day 0 to 2.5, the net flow is 50, 40, 30, 20, 10, 0 at half-day intervals. Euler uses the first five values:

change = 50(0.5) + 40(0.5) + 30(0.5) + 20(0.5) + 10(0.5) = 75.

The true graphical area is 0.5 x 2.5 x 50 = 62.5.

Answer: Forward Euler overestimates when net flow is falling over the interval because the starting flow is larger than the later flow. Here it overestimates the change by 75 - 62.5 = 12.5 units. If net flow rises, forward Euler underestimates.
Five-mark answer: equation, left-rectangle description, constant-flow/small-step assumptions, and the falling-flow explanation. Units are good practice but not a core assumption.

4. Link Polarity, Loop Polarity and Feedback 8/8 papers

Past-paper question: "Define a feedback loop, and describe the two different types of feedback. Summarise the advantages of adding feedback structures to a stock and flow model." 2024/25 Q1(a) [5]
Open workings
  • A positive link means two variables move in the same direction; a negative link means they move in opposite directions.
  • A feedback loop is a closed causal chain in which a variable eventually affects itself.
  • An even number of negative links makes a reinforcing loop. An odd number makes a balancing loop.

For Backlog +> Pressure +> Completed -> Backlog, there is one negative link. Increasing backlog ultimately increases completions, which reduces backlog: it is balancing.

Answer: Reinforcing feedback amplifies a change, for example infection creating more infection. Balancing feedback resists a discrepancy, for example backlog creating work pressure and completions. Adding feedback makes delayed side-effects and unintended policy consequences visible, rather than treating flows as independent.
Exam technique: give a definition, state the odd/even rule, then trace an increase around a named loop. A bare B or R label is not a justification.

5. Workload Stock-Flow Model and Policy Loops 8/8 papers

Past-paper question: Construct a stock and flow model of an insurance claims backlog with inflows New Claims and Reworked Claims, and outflow Claims Completed. Higher backlog raises Work Pressure. Pressure reduces Time per Claim, increases Temporary Staff and lengthens the Workweek; all raise Claims Completed. The side-effects are more Reworked Claims from shorter time per claim, more Time per Claim from temporary staff, and more Fatigue from a longer workweek which reduces Claims Completed. Identify the three policy loops and three side-effect loops. 2025/26 Q3(b,c) [12, 7]
Open workings

Claims Backlog = INTEG(New Claims + Reworked Claims - Claims Completed, Initial Backlog).

Policy loops:

  1. Backlog + Pressure + lower Time per Claim + Completed - Backlog: balancing.
  2. Backlog + Pressure + Temporary Staff + Completed - Backlog: balancing.
  3. Backlog + Pressure + Workweek + Completed - Backlog: balancing.

Side-effects: shorter Time per Claim raises Reworked Claims; more Temporary Staff raises Time per Claim and lowers Completed; a longer Workweek raises Fatigue and lowers Completed. Each feeds backlog back up and is reinforcing.

Answer: The policies reduce backlog in the short run, but the side-effects can create a delayed increase in backlog. The best recommendation is not simply to increase the workweek; compare immediate balancing benefit with quality, training and fatigue costs, and use the model to test combined policies.
What to draw: one stock, its three named flows, all stated auxiliaries, information arrows, link signs and loop labels. Do not replace actual flows with circles.

6. Fractional Flows and Adjustment to a Goal 6/8 papers

Past-paper question: "Describe, showing relevant examples, the following flows: Fractional Increase Rate; Fractional Decrease Rate; Adjustment to a Goal." 2024/25 Q2(a) [6]
Open workings
Increase = Stock x fractional increase rate
Decrease = Stock x fractional decrease rate
Adjustment = (Goal - Actual) / adjustment time
  • A fractional increase/decrease rate has units 1/time; multiplying by the stock gives a flow in units/time.
  • Fractional growth or decline gives exponential behaviour when the fraction is constant.
  • Adjustment to a goal is positive below the goal, negative above it and zero at the goal, so it is a balancing structure.
Answer: For a customer stock of 1,000 with gain fraction 10%/year, Customers Gained is 100/year. With loss fraction 3%/year, Customers Lost is 30/year. In a goal-seeking model, a target stock of 500 and actual stock of 450 with adjustment time 5 gives adjustment (500 - 450)/5 = 10 units/year.
Minute detail: a fraction is not itself a flow. Write the stock multiplication, give units, and say how the sign of the discrepancy changes the adjustment.

7. Stock Management: Anchor and Adjustment 8/8 papers

Past-paper question: "Explain how the adjustment to a goal structure can be extended to the stock management structure. When explaining the stock management structure describe: (1) the anchor and (2) the adjustment. Show the delay structure that is used to construct the anchor." 2024/25 Q2(b) [7]
Open workings

Adjustment: correct the gap between desired and actual stock: (Desired Stock - Actual Stock) / adjustment time.

Anchor: replace expected losses so that a stock already at target stays at target: Expected Losses = SMOOTH(Losses, expectation adjustment time).

Total inflow: Required Inflow = Expected Losses + Adjustment.

At equilibrium, Actual = Desired, so Adjustment = 0 and Required Inflow = Expected Losses. The anchor is a smoothing/delay structure; the adjustment is a balancing goal-seeking structure.

Answer: A simple goal-seeking model only corrects a gap. Stock management also anticipates normal outflows. The anchor prevents the stock drifting below target while the adjustment responds to a new discrepancy.
Key distinction: anchor = expected replacement flow, and adjustment = correction of target gap. Both should appear explicitly.

8. Staff Recruitment as Stock Management 7/8 papers

Past-paper question: Design a stock and flow model with equations for University staff recruitment as a stock management structure. Staff starts at 500 in equilibrium; desired staff is based on Students and a desired student-to-staff ratio of 20. Students start in equilibrium at 10,000. At time 10, the desired ratio drops from 20 to 10. Plot staff to time 30 and identify the auxiliary that controls the speed of return to steady state. 2024/25 Q2(c) [12]
Open workings
Desired Staff = Students / Desired Student Staff Ratio
Staff = INTEG(Hires - Quits, 500)
Quits = quit fraction x Staff
Expected Quits = SMOOTH(Quits, expectation adjustment time)
Hires = Expected Quits + (Desired Staff - Staff) / staff adjustment time

Initially 10,000 / 20 = 500, so the discrepancy is zero. At time 10, desired staff becomes 10,000 / 10 = 1,000. Hires exceed quits; staff rises smoothly and then settles at 1,000.

Answer: The staff curve is flat at 500 until time 10, then rises towards 1,000 with a decreasing slope and settles at the new target. The staff adjustment time has the largest direct effect on how quickly this happens. Smaller adjustment time means faster correction, but can create unrealistic hiring rates.
Full answer needs: staff stock, hires and quits, student-to-staff ratio, desired staff, expected losses/anchor, discrepancy/adjustment and the balancing feedback loop.

9. Effects Structure for Student Attrition 5/8 papers

Past-paper question: Construct equations for the effect of the student-to-staff ratio on student attrition. The reference ratio is 20 and reference attrition is 0.02. At ratio 30, attrition is five times its reference value; at ratio 15, it is half its reference value. State assumptions and show the effect relationship on an x-y plot. 2016/17 Q4(b) [15]; also 2018/19 Q4(b)
Open workings
Student Staff Ratio = Students / Staff
Normalised Ratio = Student Staff Ratio / 20
Attrition Fraction = 0.02 x Effect of Ratio(Normalised Ratio)
Attritions = Students x Attrition Fraction

The lookup/effect plot must pass through: (0.75, 0.5), (1.0, 1.0), and (1.5, 5.0). The first coordinate is normalised ratio; the second is an effect multiplier.

Therefore: ratio 15 gives 0.02 x 0.5 = 0.01; ratio 20 gives 0.02; ratio 30 gives 0.02 x 5 = 0.10.

Answer: Use a dimensionless effect multiplier rather than directly guessing a nonlinear attrition equation. State how you interpolate between the three specified points and what happens outside the stated range, for example cap the effect to avoid impossible attrition fractions.
Common error: do not put 15, 20 and 30 directly on a normalised effect graph. Divide the ratio by its reference value first.

10. SIR Numerical Calculations 8/8 papers

Past-paper question: "Suppose we have a town with 10,000 (=N) individuals, of which 2% were infectious with measles, with R0 = 12 and D = 7 days. Calculate the force of infection lambda. Calculate the Herd Immunity Threshold." 2024/25 Q3(a) [5]
Open workings

I/N = 0.02. With R0 = beta x D, the effective contact rate is:

beta = R0/D = 12/7 = 1.714 per day.

lambda = beta x I/N = (12/7)(0.02) = 0.0343 per day.

HIT = 1 - 1/R0 = 1 - 1/12 = 0.9167, or 91.67%.

Answer: The force of infection is approximately 0.0343/day, meaning a susceptible person faces about a 3.43% infection hazard per day in this simplified model. The herd immunity threshold is approximately 91.7%.
Do not confuse: lambda is a per-susceptible rate; the total infection flow is lambda x S people/day.

11. Core SIR Stocks, Flows and Feedbacks 8/8 papers

Past-paper question: "Show the Stocks, Flows and Feedbacks for the SIR Model. Show the structure and polarity of the following loops: Contagion, Depletion and Recovery. Explain the formulation of the number of infections (inflow to Infectious)." 2018/19 Q3(b) [12]
Open workings

Draw [Susceptible] -- Infections --> [Infectious] -- Recoveries --> [Recovered].

lambda = c x i x I / N
Infections = lambda x S = c x i x I x S / N
Recoveries = I / D
S = INTEG(-Infections, S0)
I = INTEG(Infections - Recoveries, I0)
R = INTEG(Recoveries, R0)
  • Contagion: I increases infections, which increases I: reinforcing.
  • Depletion: I increases infections, which lowers S, which lowers infections and I: balancing.
  • Recovery: I increases recoveries, which lowers I: balancing.
Answer: I/N is the infectious proportion encountered by a susceptible person; c is contacts per day and i is the probability a contact transmits. Thus infections have units people/day. R0 = c x i x D when the population is fully susceptible.
Full-mark detail: use three rectangles for the stocks, pipe/valve flows between them, causal arrows for the loops, and explain every factor in the infection equation.

12. R0, Equilibrium and the Infection Peak 8/8 papers

Past-paper question: "Show that when an outbreak is at its peak (dI/dt = 0), the proportion susceptible equals 1/R0. Explain why the SIR model conforms to the statement: the chain of transmission eventually breaks down due to a decline in infectives, not due to a complete lack of susceptibles." 2018/19 Q3(c) [6]
Open workings

At the peak, infections equal recoveries:

beta x I x S/N = I/D

For I > 0, divide by I:

beta x S/N = 1/D

Multiply by D and use R0 = beta x D:

R0 x S/N = 1, therefore S/N = 1/R0.

Answer: The infection curve stops rising when each infectious person generates one new infection on average. Susceptibles remain after the peak, but their proportion is too low to sustain growth. Recovery then continues to remove infectious people, so infections decline.
Related equilibrium result: if S=N, equilibrium requires beta D = 1, equivalently c i d = 1. This is the outbreak threshold.

13. SIR Countermeasures: Quarantine and Vaccination 8/8 papers

Past-paper question: Extend the SIR model with a quarantine stock and a vaccinated stock. Twenty percent of new infections enter quarantine and recover after two days; quarantined people do not infect others. Only susceptible people are vaccinated through a fractional decrease flow. Vaccination fraction rises from 0 towards 0.15 by goal seeking. Let R0 = 2, N = 10,000 and one person initially infected. Specify all equations, show feedbacks, and include overall attack rate. 2025/26 Q2(b) [12]
Open workings
beta = R0/D = 2/2 = 1 per day
lambda = beta x I/N
To I = (1 - QF) x lambda x S
To Q = QF x lambda x S
Vaccinations = Vaccination Fraction x S
Vaccination Fraction = INTEG((0.15 - Vaccination Fraction)/AT, 0)
Recoveries I = I/2; Recoveries Q = Q/2

S = INTEG(-To I - To Q - Vaccinations, 9999); I = INTEG(To I - Recoveries I, 1); Q = INTEG(To Q - Recoveries Q, 0); R = INTEG(Recoveries I + Recoveries Q, 0); V = INTEG(Vaccinations, 0).

Attack Rate = (I + Q + R)/N. This counts people currently or previously infected, not vaccinated people.

Answer: Quarantine reduces transmission immediately by diverting part of the infection flow into a non-transmitting stock. Vaccination removes susceptible people, strengthening the depletion feedback. Higher quarantine fraction and higher final vaccination fraction should both reduce final attack rate; the smallest attack-rate circles belong in the high-quarantine/high-vaccination corner.
Common loss of marks: do not move people from Infectious to Quarantine if the paper states that a fraction of the infection flow enters quarantine. Do not allow Vaccinated people to remain in the infection flow.

14. Little's Law and Delay Interpretation 3 papers

Past-paper question: "Describe Little's Law, and why it is useful in helping understand the behaviour of systems in equilibrium. If a broadband provider has a pending pool of 5,000 installations and installs an average of 2,500 per month, what is the average waiting time (assume equilibrium)? Draw a stock and flow model to illustrate your answer." 2017/18 Q4(a,b) [3, 3]
Open workings

Stock = Throughput x Average Delay, so Average Delay = Stock / Throughput.

Delay = 5,000 installations / 2,500 installations per month = 2 months.

Draw Installations Waiting as the stock, with an inflow of installations requested and outflow of installations completed. Little's Law applies when the stock is in equilibrium: average inflow equals average outflow and the average stock is stable.

Answer: The average customer waits two months. Little's Law relates average stock, average throughput and average time in the system without requiring the exact individual delay distribution. A smooth delayed outflow generally indicates a distributed delay rather than every item leaving after one identical fixed delay.
Condition to state: this is an average steady-state relationship, not a claim that every individual waits exactly two months.
