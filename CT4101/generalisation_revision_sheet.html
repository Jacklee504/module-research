CT4101 Topic Guide

Generalisation, Bias and Variance

Diagnose why a model succeeds on its training data but fails on new cases.

Exam-use protocol

Before writing, identify the command word: calculate/trace requires an ordered derivation; explain requires a definition, mechanism and assumption; compare/design requires matched criteria, a justified choice and a limitation. The topic-specific recall and worked routes below are the material to adapt—not text to copy blindly.

Part-by-part recall: definitions, assumptions and mechanics

Definitions: high bias underfits (train/validation both poor); high variance overfits (training good, validation worse). Dimensionality makes data sparse and distances less informative. Question cue: diagnose from both curves/errors then match a remedy.

Worked practice 1 — paper-style synthesis (recurring-topic pattern; not a quoted paper prompt) diagnosis

Question demand: 98% training, 65% validation accuracy. Plan/choice: compare gap. Worked answer: high variance; try regularisation, simpler model, more representative data/early stopping and confirm on validation. Mark-winning points: evidence for diagnosis. Common trap: all low test scores mean overfit.

Worked practice 2 — synthesis / design

Question demand: Mitigate curse of dimensionality for kNN. Plan/choice: remove/noise-reduce features and validate k/metric. Worked answer: scale features, use domain selection/PCA where defensible, tune k by CV and report held-out performance; extra irrelevant dimensions corrupt neighbour similarity. Mark-winning points: method reason. Common trap: more features always help.

Purpose and relevance

Generalisation is performance on unseen data from the intended population. Underfitting is too simple to learn the pattern; overfitting memorises quirks rather than the signal.

What a question really asks — answer plan

A learning-curve question asks you to compare training and validation/test error, diagnose bias or variance, and give a remedy that targets that diagnosis.

Prerequisites, vocabulary and formulas

  • Bias: systematic error from overly restrictive assumptions.
  • Variance: sensitivity to which training examples were sampled.
  • Underfit/high bias: training and validation errors both high and close.
  • Overfit/high variance: training error low, validation error notably higher.
  • Curse of dimensionality: in many dimensions, data become sparse and distances less informative.

Step-by-step method

  1. Read training performance first.
  2. Compare the gap to validation performance.
  3. Label the pattern: high bias or high variance.
  4. Choose a matching remedy: more expressive features/model for bias; regularisation, simplification, more data, or early stopping for variance.

Worked practice 3 — foundational calculation

Question: A model has 98% training accuracy and 65% validation accuracy. Diagnose it.

Model answer: The 33-point gap suggests overfitting/high variance: it fits training-specific details but generalises poorly. Try regularisation, a simpler model, early stopping, more representative data, and validate the effect using a held-out procedure.

Question interpretation and independent practice

Prompt 1

Both training and validation accuracy are 55%. Check: likely underfitting/high bias; try a richer representation or less restrictive model.

Exam bridge

Sketch and explain learning curves for underfitting and overfitting, then recommend one remedy for each. Check: describe curve gaps, not just labels; remedies must differ. More data commonly helps variance but may not fix high bias.

High-value traps and corrections

  • Calling every low test score overfitting.
  • Claiming more features always improve generalisation.
  • Using test data repeatedly to diagnose and tune.

Final answer checklist

  • Did I compare training and validation?
  • Did I identify bias versus variance?
  • Does my remedy match the diagnosis?
