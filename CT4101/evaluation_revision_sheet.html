CT4101 Topic Guide

Evaluation Metrics and ROC

Measure whether a model is useful, not merely whether it produces an answer.

Exam-use protocol

Before writing, identify the command word: calculate/trace requires an ordered derivation; explain requires a definition, mechanism and assumption; compare/design requires matched criteria, a justified choice and a limitation. The topic-specific recall and worked routes below are the material to adapt—not text to copy blindly.

Part-by-part recall: definitions, assumptions and mechanics

Definitions: TP, FP, TN, FN drive precision, recall, specificity and F1=2PR/(P+R). ROC plots TPR against FPR across thresholds; imbalance makes accuracy deceptive. Question cue: declare positive class/cost before calculation.

Worked practice 1 — paper-style synthesis (recurring-topic pattern; not a quoted paper prompt) confusion matrix

Question demand: Calculate precision and recall from TP=40, FP=10, FN=20. Plan/choice: use prediction-positive versus actual-positive denominators. Worked answer: P=40/50=.8; R=40/60=.667. Mark-winning points: substituted denominators and practical interpretation. Common trap: swap them.

Worked practice 2 — synthesis / design

Question demand: Set a screening threshold for rare disease. Plan/choice: compare threshold points/ROC or PR under false-negative cost. Worked answer: choose high recall subject to manageable false positives, report precision and capacity impact, then validate on held-out prevalence-matched data. Mark-winning points: threshold/cost trade-off. Common trap: best accuracy.

Purpose and relevance

Evaluation turns predictions into evidence. The right metric follows the error that matters: missing disease differs from incorrectly flagging a healthy person.

What a question really asks — answer plan

For a confusion-matrix question: choose the positive class, fill TP/FP/TN/FN, substitute into formulas, then interpret the metric in the domain. For ROC, label axes before discussing thresholds.

Prerequisites, vocabulary and formulas

  • TP: positive predicted positive; FP: negative predicted positive; TN: negative predicted negative; FN: positive predicted negative.
  • Accuracy=(TP+TN)/all; precision=TP/(TP+FP); recall/TPR=TP/(TP+FN).
  • FPR=FP/(FP+TN); F1=2·precision·recall/(precision+recall).
  • ROC plots TPR (y) against FPR (x) over thresholds; AUC nearer 1 is generally better.

Step-by-step method

  1. State which class is positive.
  2. Make a 2×2 table; do not calculate from prose alone.
  3. Use the requested denominator.
  4. Explain whether the result is good in context and why accuracy can mislead on imbalance.

Worked practice 3 — foundational calculation

Question: TP=8, FP=2, TN=85, FN=5. Find precision and recall.

Model answer: Precision=8/(8+2)=0.80: 80% of positive alerts were right. Recall=8/(8+5)=0.615: the model found about 61.5% of actual positives.

Question interpretation and independent practice

Prompt 1

Which metric prioritises finding almost every true positive? Check: recall; it penalises false negatives.

Exam bridge

Explain an ROC curve and how changing a classification threshold creates a trade-off. Check: axes are FPR and TPR; lowering threshold usually increases both; the top-left region is desirable; AUC summarises ranking over thresholds.

High-value traps and corrections

  • Swapping precision and recall denominators.
  • Using accuracy as proof of a good rare-event model.
  • Drawing ROC axes as precision versus recall.

Final answer checklist

  • Did I choose the positive class?
  • Did I show substitutions?
  • Did I interpret the practical error?
