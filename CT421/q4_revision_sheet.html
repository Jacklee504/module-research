Explainability and Bio-Inspired AI

Five recurring families: explainability, artificial life and emergence, neuro-evolution, ant colony optimisation, and symbolic versus connectionist AI.

What repeatedly appears in the papers

Question 4 changes its mix, but its demands are precise. The papers ask for a mechanism and an application, not a list of definitions. Explainability appears in every local paper from 2021/22 to 2024/25; artificial life appears from 2021/22 to 2023/24; neuro-evolution appears in 2021/22 and 2022/23; ACO appears in 2023/24 and 2024/25; symbolic versus connectionist AI appears in 2024/25.

How to answer a Q4 part

  1. Circle the verb. Explain needs a causal account; describe needs ordered components; compare needs matched criteria; show how needs the supplied example carried through the mechanism.
  2. Use the context in the question. A generic definition of ACO does not show how it selects a route in the stated network.
  3. Spend marks on distinct points. For an 8-mark comparison, define both sides, apply both to one domain, then give a grounded benefit and limitation for each.
  4. End with a limitation or validation condition where the wording asks for real-world use. That is where explainability, evolved controllers and learning systems become defensible rather than merely named.

Practice question 1 - explainability in deep learning [9 marks]

Inspired by: 2024/25 Q4(c), with the repeated explainability family in 2021/22 Q4(c), 2022/23 Q4(a) and 2023/24 Q4(a).

Scenario. DermAssist predicts whether a skin-lesion image needs urgent specialist review. A clinician sees a high-risk prediction for a lesion image, but the network has millions of learned weights and the patient’s image includes a ruler, a shadow and a skin marker.

Explain what “black box” means here and why it is problematic. Describe two different ways DermAssist could make this prediction more interpretable. [9 marks]

Question-demand diagnosis: this is not asking whether the network is accurate. It requires (1) an account of opacity, (2) real consequences in the stated high-stakes setting, and (3) two different explanation approaches applied to this image.

Answer route: define opacity in terms of inaccessible distributed representations; identify safety, accountability and shortcut-learning risks; then give one image-attribution method and one local surrogate or example-based method, including a fidelity caveat.

Worked answer: DermAssist is a black box because its output is produced by many interacting learned weights rather than a small, inspectable chain of rules. A high predicted risk therefore does not itself reveal whether the network used lesion structure, the ruler, the shadow, or a spurious pattern in the training set. This is problematic because a clinician must decide whether to act on the alert, developers need to detect bias or shortcuts, and the service needs an auditable reason for a potentially harmful false negative or false positive.

A saliency method such as Grad-CAM can overlay the image regions that most increased the high-risk score. If the heatmap is concentrated on the lesion rather than the ruler or marker, that is useful evidence that the model attended to clinically relevant image content; if it highlights the ruler, the team should investigate the dataset and retrain or constrain the model. A second approach is a local surrogate explanation around this particular image: perturb clinically meaningful inputs or compare similar historical images, then fit a small interpretable model that approximates why this prediction changed. It can communicate that irregular border and colour variation raised the local score, while a benign-looking reference image lowered it. Neither output proves causation or fully explains the entire neural network, so the team should test explanation stability and fidelity and keep clinical review in the decision loop.

Topic-specific trap: do not say a heatmap makes the full network transparent. It is a local, post-hoc account that can itself be misleading or unstable.

Practice question 2 - ant colony optimisation [8 marks]

Inspired by: 2024/25 Q4(a) and 2023/24 Q4(c).

Scenario. A delivery robot must travel from depot S to clinic T. It can use route S-A-T (total length 6) or S-B-T (total length 10). At the start, all four edges have equal pheromone. The heuristic favours a shorter next edge; after a complete route, a shorter route deposits more pheromone than a longer route.

Explain the key principles of ACO, including pheromones and evaporation. Show how repeated ants could use this network to favour a short route from S to T. [8 marks]

Question-demand diagnosis: “Show how” requires the supplied two-route network, not only an ant metaphor. The answer must include construction, evaluation, reinforcement and evaporation.

Answer route: first state the probabilistic choice rule in words, then trace an initial iteration and a later iteration. Explain why evaporation keeps S-B-T possible if conditions change.

Worked answer: In ACO, each artificial ant builds a candidate solution one step at a time. At S it chooses A or B probabilistically: a stronger pheromone trail and a better heuristic value make an edge more likely, but they do not make it compulsory. Initially the pheromones are equal, so the shorter-edge heuristic can make S-A attractive while some ants still explore S-B. Each ant completes a route and its route length is evaluated. An ant that used S-A-T receives a better score because its total is 6, so it deposits more pheromone on S-A and A-T than an ant on the 10-unit S-B-T route deposits on its two edges.

On later iterations, ants at S are biased by both the shorter-edge heuristic and the reinforced S-A pheromone, so S-A-T is selected increasingly often. Evaporation reduces pheromone on all edges before or alongside new deposits. It prevents an early accidental preference from becoming permanent and leaves some chance to explore S-B-T. If congestion later made S-A-T poor, its reduced reinforcement and continued evaporation would allow the alternative to recover. Thus pheromone exploits repeatedly good route components, while evaporation maintains exploration.

Topic-specific trap: pheromone is not a physical guarantee that every ant follows the best route. ACO retains stochastic choice and can converge prematurely if exploration is too weak.

Practice question 3 - neuro-evolution [9 marks]

Inspired by: 2021/22 Q4(b) and 2022/23 Q4(b).

Scenario. A warehouse rover has distance sensors and must learn to reach a charging bay without collisions. A simulator can score each controller by distance progressed, energy used and a large collision penalty, but it does not provide a differentiable gradient through contacts and the map changes between episodes.

Describe what is meant by neuro-evolution. Give a suitable representation for the rover’s neural network and discuss advantages and limitations of the approach. [9 marks]

Question-demand diagnosis: this has three mark-bearing parts: evolutionary optimisation of a network, a concrete encoding, and balanced advantages and limitations in the supplied sparse/noisy simulator.

Answer route: encode a feed-forward controller, define fitness from the scenario, then explain selection and variation. Link the lack of gradients to an advantage and repeated simulation to the principal cost.

Worked answer: Neuro-evolution evolves neural networks using an evolutionary algorithm rather than updating weights through back-propagated gradients. A population contains candidate rover controllers. Each controller is run on several maps; its fitness can reward progress toward the charger, subtract energy use, and apply a strong penalty for collision. Higher-fitness controllers are selected more often for reproduction. Mutation changes some connection weights, and crossover can combine compatible parent weight vectors; the resulting offspring are evaluated in a new generation.

For a fixed architecture with sensor inputs, hidden units and steering/throttle outputs, a suitable chromosome is one ordered vector containing every connection weight and bias. The decoder places each number back on its named connection. A richer alternative is a graph encoding that records nodes and edges, allowing mutations to add a connection or hidden unit as well as changing weights. The fixed vector is simpler to crossover; the graph can evolve topology but needs bookkeeping to preserve meaningful alignment between parents.

An advantage here is that the simulator’s collision dynamics and changing maps need not be differentiable: the only requirement is a fitness score. Evolution can also search architecture and weights together and can reward delayed behaviour, such as reaching the charger only after many moves. Its limitation is expense and noisy evaluation: many full simulation episodes are required for each generation, and a controller can overfit a small set of maps or exploit an unintended scoring loophole. Evaluating on varied held-out maps, using several runs per controller and preserving diverse candidates reduces, but does not remove, that cost.

Topic-specific trap: “a genetic algorithm trains a neural network” is incomplete unless the chromosome says what is encoded and fitness says how a candidate controller is judged.

Practice question 4 - artificial life and emergence [8 marks]

Inspired by: 2021/22 Q4(a), 2022/23 Q4(c) and 2023/24 Q4(b).

Scenario. In a Boids-style coastal-drone simulation, each drone can sense only nearby drones. At every time step it applies three rules: steer away when too close (separation), steer toward the average nearby heading (alignment), and steer toward the average nearby position (cohesion). There is no leader and no global flight plan.

Using this existing system, explain the main properties of an artificial-life system and show how complex behaviour can emerge from the simple local rules. Give one example of how a naturally occurring system has informed a useful algorithm. [8 marks]

Question-demand diagnosis: name properties through the Boids system, then connect local rules to a population-level result. The final request needs a second nature-to-algorithm example, not merely another Boids sentence.

Answer route: identify decentralisation, local interaction and dynamic adaptation; trace what each rule prevents or encourages; name flocking as an unprogrammed global pattern; finish with ants informing ACO.

Worked answer: Artificial life studies life-like behaviour in computational systems, especially populations of autonomous entities that interact locally and adapt their behaviour over time. The Boids drones are decentralised: no controller calculates a formation for the group. Each drone observes only a neighbourhood and changes its own velocity from separation, alignment and cohesion. Separation prevents collisions, alignment tends to make neighbours travel in a similar direction, and cohesion resists the group fragmenting.

Repeated local updates can produce coherent flocking, turns and splits around obstacles. No rule says “make a V-shaped flock” or assigns a leader; that organised whole-population pattern emerges from the interaction of the simpler individual rules. The system is therefore useful for explaining emergence: global order is an outcome of local feedback, not a centrally stored plan. It can also be explored under changed density, sensing radius or noise to see which collective properties remain stable.

A naturally occurring system can directly inform an algorithm. Ant foraging inspired ACO: artificial ants construct routes, successful routes receive stronger pheromone, and pheromone evaporation prevents early lock-in. In routing or travelling-salesperson search this turns a biological collective-search idea into a method for finding good solutions. The algorithm is inspired by the mechanism; it is not a claim that software ants reproduce every detail of real ant biology.

Topic-specific trap: an emergent flock is not random. It is structured behaviour generated by rules, even though no single drone represents the whole pattern.

Practice question 5 - symbolic versus connectionist AI [8 marks]

Inspired by: 2024/25 Q4(b).

Scenario. A hospital wants a triage assistant. It receives a clinician-entered symptom list, vital signs and a chest X-ray. Its recommendations must be reviewable by clinicians, but the hospital also has many labelled X-rays containing subtle visual signs that are difficult to express as hand-written rules.

Compare and contrast symbolic AI and connectionist AI in this application. Discuss an advantage and limitation of each approach. [8 marks]

Question-demand diagnosis: compare the two paradigms against the same triage task. A two-column list earns less than applying representation, learning, explanation and failure modes to the hospital’s inputs.

Answer route: define an explicit-rule system and a neural network, apply each to the appropriate information source, then make one balanced deployment conclusion rather than declaring a winner.

Worked answer: Symbolic AI represents knowledge explicitly, for example as rules such as “if oxygen saturation is below a threshold and respiratory rate is high, raise an urgent-review recommendation.” An inference engine can combine facts entered by the clinician and report which rules fired. Its advantage in triage is auditability: a clinician can inspect the conditions, challenge an outdated threshold and verify why the recommendation was produced. Its limitation is knowledge-engineering effort and brittleness. It is difficult to write complete rules for the subtle visual texture of an X-ray, and unanticipated combinations of symptoms may fall outside its rule base.

Connectionist AI uses a neural network with learned, distributed weights. A convolutional model can learn visual patterns from the labelled X-rays and may detect weak image cues that a rule author cannot easily specify. That is a practical advantage for high-dimensional, noisy visual data. Its limitations are dependence on representative labelled data and reduced transparency: a high-risk score may be affected by a scanner artefact or demographic imbalance, while the learned weights do not provide a clinician-readable rule. The hospital can combine the approaches: a neural model can analyse the X-ray while symbolic safety rules check vital-sign thresholds and route all recommendations to human review. This hybrid design preserves explicit safeguards without pretending that the neural result has become fully explainable.

Topic-specific trap: do not equate symbolic AI with “old” or connectionist AI with “always more accurate.” The appropriate choice depends on data, required reasoning, auditability and the cost of error.
