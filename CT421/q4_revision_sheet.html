The recurring Question 4 material: explainability, artificial life, neuro-evolution, ant colony optimisation, and symbolic versus connectionist AI.

Before writing, identify the command word: calculate/trace requires an ordered derivation; explain requires a definition, mechanism and assumption; compare/design requires matched criteria, a justified choice and a limitation. The topic-specific recall and worked routes below are the material to adapt—not text to copy blindly.

Part-by-part recall: definitions, assumptions and mechanics

Definitions: explainability communicates why a model output arose; ACO builds solutions probabilistically from pheromone and heuristic information, then reinforces good paths and evaporates old trails. Compare: symbolic models expose rules; connectionist models can learn complex patterns but are harder to inspect. Question cue: explain mechanism, application and limitation.

Worked practice 1 — paper-derived ACO

Question demand: Describe one ACO iteration for routing. Plan/choice: construction, evaluation, reinforcement and evaporation. Worked answer: ants choose edges by pheromone/heuristic probability, score complete routes, deposit more pheromone on good routes, evaporate all trails to prevent lock-in. Mark-winning points: all four stages. Common trap: pheromone only increases.

Worked practice 2 — synthesis / design

Question demand: Choose an explanation for a high-stakes classifier. Plan/choice: distinguish global model understanding from local decision explanation and validate usefulness. Worked answer: provide auditable feature/decision explanation appropriate to user, test fidelity/stability and disclose limits; a post-hoc explanation is not proof the black box is causal. Mark-winning points: audience and limitation. Common trap: one explanation makes model transparent.

Explainability

Black-box models

A black-box model makes predictions accurately but does not make its reasoning easy for humans to inspect. Deep neural networks are often called black boxes because many learned weights interact in complex ways.

Why it matters: explanations support trust, debugging, bias detection, accountability, and safe use in high-stakes domains such as healthcare, lending, and law.

Ways to explain deep learning

  • Feature importance: show which input features most affected a prediction.
  • Saliency or attention maps: highlight image regions or tokens that influenced the output.
  • Local surrogate explanations: approximate one prediction with a simpler interpretable model.
  • Interpretable-by-design models: use simpler rules or symbolic components where explanation is more important than raw accuracy.

Artificial Life and Neuro-Evolution

Artificial life

Artificial life studies life-like behaviour in simulated systems. Its main ideas are local interaction, adaptation, decentralisation, and emergence: complex global behaviour can arise from simple individual rules.

Example: In the Boids model, simple separation, alignment, and cohesion rules create flocking behaviour without a leader. The Game of Life similarly produces complex patterns from simple cell rules.

Neuro-evolution

Neuro-evolution uses an evolutionary algorithm to optimise a neural network's weights, architecture, or both.

  • Representation: a chromosome can be a vector of weights or a graph encoding nodes and connections.
  • Advantages: does not require gradients, can optimise architecture, and can work with delayed or sparse rewards.
  • Limitations: evaluating many networks is computationally expensive and can be slow for large networks.

Ant Colony Optimisation

Ant Colony Optimisation (ACO) is a search method inspired by ants finding routes. Artificial ants construct solutions probabilistically, preferring paths with stronger pheromone trails and often shorter distances. Good solutions deposit pheromone; evaporation reduces old pheromone so the search does not get stuck too early.

Example: For a shortest-path or travelling-salesperson problem, ants repeatedly build paths. Shorter routes deposit more pheromone, making them more likely to be selected in later iterations.

Symbolic versus Connectionist AI

  • Symbolic AI: represents knowledge with rules and symbols. It is interpretable and supports explicit reasoning, but can be brittle and difficult to scale from raw data.
  • Connectionist AI: learns patterns using neural networks. It handles noisy, high-dimensional data well, but generally needs more data and is harder to explain.

Question interpretation and independent practice

  • Start with a one-sentence definition, then explain the mechanism. For ACO, that means ants construct paths, pheromone reinforces good ones, and evaporation preserves exploration.
  • Use the example named in the question to prove understanding: Boids for emergence, a network path for ACO, or medical diagnosis for symbolic versus connectionist AI.
  • For compare-and-contrast questions, give one strength and one limitation of each approach. For explainability, include why it matters and two concrete explanation methods.

Exam Practice: Question 4

Question 1: Explainability in deep learning (2021/22 to 2024/25, 8-9 marks)

Question: Explain why black-box deep learning models can be problematic and describe two ways to make them more interpretable.

Short model answer:

  • A black-box model is difficult to understand internally, even when accurate.
  • This is problematic for trust, bias detection, debugging, accountability, and high-stakes decisions.
  • Feature-importance or saliency methods show which inputs influenced one output.
  • Local surrogate models explain one prediction with a simpler approximation. These explanations help, but may not fully describe the whole model.

Question 2: Ant Colony Optimisation (2023/24 and 2024/25, 8-9 marks)

Question: Explain ACO, including pheromones and evaporation, and show how it can find a short path.

Short model answer:

  • Ants construct candidate paths, biased by pheromone strength and path quality.
  • After each iteration, good or short paths deposit more pheromone.
  • Evaporation reduces old pheromone, preserving exploration and preventing early paths from dominating forever.
  • Over many iterations, short routes receive more pheromone and become more likely to be selected.

Question 3: Neuro-evolution (2021/22 and 2022/23, 9 marks)

Question: Explain neuro-evolution, including representation, advantages, and limitations.

Short model answer:

  • Neuro-evolution evolves neural-network weights, architecture, or both using a GA.
  • A chromosome can be a list of connection weights or a graph of nodes and edges.
  • It works without gradients and can optimise architectures or sparse-reward tasks.
  • It is expensive because many candidate networks must be evaluated, especially for large networks.

Question 4: Artificial life (2021/22, 2022/23, and 2023/24, 8 marks)

Question: With reference to an existing system, explain the main properties of an artificial-life system. Include an example of complex behaviour emerging from simple rules.

Short model answer:

  • Artificial life simulates life-like properties such as decentralised interaction, adaptation, evolution, and emergence.
  • In Boids, each agent follows only separation, alignment, and cohesion rules.
  • Despite no central controller, the group displays flocking behaviour. This is emergence: complex global behaviour arising from simple local rules.
  • Algorithms such as ACO use this idea by copying useful properties of a natural system.

Question 5: Symbolic versus connectionist AI (2024/25, 8 marks)

Question: Compare symbolic AI and connectionist AI. Using one application, discuss an advantage and limitation of each.

Short model answer:

  • Symbolic AI uses explicit rules and symbols. It is interpretable, but manually built rule systems can be brittle.
  • Connectionist AI learns patterns using neural networks. It handles raw, noisy data well, but usually needs much more training data and is harder to explain.
  • For medical diagnosis, a rule system can explain its conclusion clearly; a neural network may detect subtle patterns in scans but needs explanation methods to justify a decision.

High-value traps and final review

Worked example: one ACO update

Problem: Two routes have equal heuristic desirability, but route A has stronger pheromone after a shorter previous tour.

Model reasoning: An ant is more likely to select A because stronger pheromone biases construction toward previously good routes. After tours are evaluated, short routes add more pheromone; evaporation then reduces every trail so an early route cannot dominate forever. Thus reinforcement exploits quality while evaporation preserves exploration.

Common mistakes

  • Describing pheromone reinforcement but omitting evaporation.
  • Calling emergence a centrally planned behaviour.
  • Claiming an explanation method makes a black-box model fully transparent.

  • For ACO, include construction, reinforcement, and evaporation.
