Genetic Algorithms

The recurring Question 2 material: representation, fitness, selection, crossover, mutation, schema theorem, and exploration versus exploitation.

Exam-use protocol

Before writing, identify the command word: calculate/trace requires an ordered derivation; explain requires a definition, mechanism and assumption; compare/design requires matched criteria, a justified choice and a limitation. The topic-specific recall and worked routes below are the material to adapt—not text to copy blindly.

Part-by-part recall: definitions, assumptions and mechanics

Definitions: GA evolves encoded candidates through fitness-based selection, crossover and mutation; representation and constraint handling determine whether operators preserve valid solutions. Compare: crossover recombines, mutation maintains exploration. Question cue: justify every design choice against objective/constraints.

Worked practice 1 — paper-derived GA design

Question demand: Design a GA for timetable construction. Plan/choice: choose valid encoding, penalised fitness and repair. Worked answer: encode assignments, reward satisfied preferences and heavily penalise clashes; select, crossover compatible segments, mutate a small assignment and repair invalid timetables. Mark-winning points: fitness and constraints. Common trap: vague “optimise fitness”.

Worked practice 2 — synthesis / design

Question demand: Diagnose premature convergence. Plan/choice: link low diversity to selection/mutation and propose measured change. Worked answer: overly strong selection loses alternatives; reduce pressure, increase/adapt mutation, use diversity/elitism carefully and compare repeated-run fitness. Mark-winning points: exploration-exploitation. Common trap: mutation only makes solutions worse.

Core Genetic Algorithm

A genetic algorithm (GA) maintains a population of candidate solutions. Each generation evaluates fitness, selects parents, creates new candidates through crossover and mutation, then replaces some or all of the population.

Representation

A chromosome representation encodes one candidate solution. It must represent valid solutions clearly and allow sensible crossover and mutation.

For task allocation, chromosome position i can store the processor assigned to task i. For student-project allocation, position i can store the project assigned to student i.

Fitness function

A fitness function scores solution quality. It should reward the objective and penalise invalid solutions. For allocation, it could reward low cost or high preference satisfaction and subtract a large penalty for capacity violations or duplicate assignments.

Selection, crossover, and mutation

  • Selection: favours fitter parents. Tournament selection is a simple common approach.
  • Crossover: combines parts of two parents to create offspring. It mainly exploits good building blocks.
  • Mutation: makes a small random change to a chromosome. It introduces new variation and helps avoid stagnation.

Exploration versus exploitation

Exploration searches new areas of the solution space; mutation and diverse parent selection support it. Exploitation improves known promising areas; selection and crossover support it. Too much exploitation causes premature convergence, while too much exploration makes progress slow.

Hill climbing

Hill climbing starts with one solution and repeatedly moves to a better neighbouring solution. It strongly exploits local improvements, is simple and fast, but can get stuck at a local optimum, plateau, or ridge. Random restarts or occasional worse moves increase exploration.

Schema theorem

A schema is a pattern in chromosomes, such as 1*0*, where * means any value. Informally, the schema theorem says short, low-order, above-average schemas tend to receive more copies in future generations.

  • Selection increases above-average schemas.
  • Crossover can preserve useful schemas but may disrupt long schemas.
  • Mutation creates variation but can disrupt a schema if its rate is too high.

Question interpretation and independent practice

  1. Ask what one complete solution must decide. If it assigns every item, task, or student, use one gene per item, task, or student.
  2. Ask what the objective rewards. Sum that quantity in the fitness function: value, preference score, or low completion time.
  3. List the constraints separately. Penalise invalid solutions heavily, or repair them after crossover and mutation.
  4. Choose an operator that makes a meaningful small change. For an allocation, moving one item or task is a natural mutation.
  5. Check that crossover can produce invalid assignments. If it can, state a repair step. This is often an easy source of marks.

Exam Practice: Genetic Algorithms

Question 1: Design a GA for resource allocation (2024/25, 15 marks)

Question: Design a genetic algorithm to allocate tasks to processors with time, memory, and capability constraints.

Short model answer:

  • Chromosome: gene i gives the processor assigned to task i.
  • Fitness: reward low completion time or balanced load; apply a large penalty where processor memory or capability constraints are violated.
  • Selection: use tournament selection so fitter valid allocations are more likely to become parents.
  • Crossover: combine task assignments from two parents, then repair invalid assignments if needed.
  • Mutation: move one task to another compatible processor. This introduces alternatives and reduces premature convergence.

Question 2: Schema theorem (2021/22, 2022/23, and 2023/24, 11 marks)

Question: Explain the schema theorem and the effects of selection, crossover, and mutation.

Short model answer:

  • A schema is a pattern shared by many chromosomes, for example 1*0*.
  • Above-average, short, low-order schemas are likely to increase because selection chooses fitter solutions more often.
  • Crossover recombines useful patterns but can break a pattern if the crossover point falls inside it.
  • Mutation maintains diversity and can create useful patterns, but a high rate may destroy them.

Question 3: Exploration and exploitation (2024/25, 10 marks)

Question: Discuss exploration and exploitation with respect to genetic-algorithm operators and a hill-climbing approach.

Short model answer:

  • In a GA, selection and crossover exploit good current solutions. Mutation, random initialisation, and diversity mechanisms explore new areas.
  • Too much exploitation causes premature convergence; too much exploration makes the search almost random.
  • Hill climbing is mostly exploitation: it selects a better neighbouring solution. It can get stuck at a local optimum or plateau.
  • Random restarts or occasional worse moves give hill climbing more exploration.

Question 4: Student-project allocation (2022/23, 14 marks)

Question: A university must assign unique projects to students, where each student ranks their top ten projects. Explain how a GA could solve this problem using a fitness function, representation, and operators.

Short model answer:

  • Chromosome position i stores the project assigned to student i. Ensure no project appears twice, either through a permutation encoding or a repair step.
  • Fitness is the sum of preference scores, with high penalties for duplicate or unacceptable assignments.
  • Use tournament selection; use a permutation-preserving crossover and a swap mutation between two students.
  • Keep the best valid solution from each generation to avoid losing a strong allocation.

Question 5: Multiple knapsack (2021/22 and 2023/24, 14 marks)

Question: Items have values and weights, and must be placed in containers with fixed weight capacities. Describe a GA that maximises total value without exceeding any capacity.

Short model answer:

  • Chromosome gene i stores the container chosen for item i, or 0 if it is not selected.
  • Fitness is the total value of selected items minus a very large penalty for every overweight container.
  • Use tournament selection. One-point crossover combines parent assignments, then a repair step removes or moves items from overloaded containers.
  • Mutation moves one item to another container, adds an unselected item, or removes an item to restore capacity.

How this answer is derived: The problem says every item needs a placement decision, so use one gene per item. It asks to maximise value, so add item values in the fitness function. It also says capacities cannot be exceeded, so add a penalty and a repair step. Finally, mutation should alter one placement because moving one item is the smallest natural change to a packing solution.

High-value traps and final review

Worked example: fitness for a small allocation

Problem: A chromosome assigns three tasks to processors. It has completion cost 12 but exceeds one memory limit by 2 units; use a penalty of 10 per excess unit.

Model reasoning: Start from the objective, so lower cost is better. Penalise the violation: adjusted cost = 12 + (2 × 10) = 32. The GA should prefer a valid allocation with a lower adjusted cost; state that repair can also restore feasibility after an operator.

Common mistakes

  • Using an encoding that cannot represent every required decision.
  • Rewarding the objective but forgetting constraint penalties or repair.
  • Describing mutation without saying how it creates a valid nearby candidate.

Final checklist

  • Give representation, fitness, selection, crossover, mutation, and constraint handling.
  • Link each operator to exploration or exploitation where asked.
  • Use the wording of the problem to justify each design choice.
