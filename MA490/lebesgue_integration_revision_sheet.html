Lebesgue Integration

Build non-negative integrals from simple functions and use a.e. statements carefully.

Purpose

Define the Lebesgue integral, calculate characteristic/simple functions, and prove zero-integral consequences.Exam use: Measurability/sign, disjoint sets, coefficients and measures, exact total, and any a.e. exceptional set are shown.

Question plan

  1. Check f is measurable and non-negative, or split into positive and negative parts where appropriate.
  2. For simple functions, collect disjoint level sets.
  3. For a general non-negative function, invoke the supremum over simple functions below f.
  4. Translate an integral-zero claim into level sets {f>ε}.
  5. Question interpretation: For calculate, reduce to disjoint level sets; for define, build from simple functions; for prove zero a.e., use positive level sets and state nonnegativity.

Definitions

For s=Σai1A_i with disjoint measurable Ai, ∫s dμ=Σaiμ(Ai). For measurable f≥0, ∫f dμ is the supremum of ∫s dμ over simple 0≤s≤f.Rapid recall: For s=sum a_i 1_{A_i} on disjoint measurable sets, integral s=sum a_i mu(A_i); for f>=0 take the supremum over simple s<=f; an integrable signed f uses f^+-f^- with finite absolute integral.

Proof method

To show ∫f=0 implies f=0 a.e. for f≥0, use Em={f≥1/m}. If any Em had positive measure, then ∫f≥μ(Em)/m>0.Exam workflow: Make level sets disjoint, compute their measures, and for abstract proofs convert pointwise positivity into E_m={f>=1/m} before applying monotonicity.

Worked simple example

For f=3·1[0,2] under Lebesgue measure, ∫f=3·λ([0,2])=6.

Medium/hard paper-style synthesis

On ([0,1],Lebesgue), let f=2 on [0,1/2), 3 on [1/3,1], and 0 otherwise, with values added on overlap. Disjointise: f=2 on [0,1/3), 5 on [1/3,1/2), and 3 on [1/2,1]. Hence integral f=2(1/3)+5(1/6)+3(1/2)=2/3+5/6+3/2=3. The naive sum 2(1/2)+3(2/3)=3 agrees by linearity, but disjoint levels expose the actual simple-function values.

Graduated exam bridges

1. Basic: Compute ∫1Adμ. Check: μ(A).

2. Exam: Why is non-negativity needed in “integral zero implies zero a.e.”? Check: signed positive and negative contributions can cancel.

Hard paper-style synthesis

Paper-style synthesis: prove that if f>=0 measurable and integral f=0, then f=0 a.e. Check E_m={f>=1/m}; 0=integral f >= mu(E_m)/m gives mu(E_m)=0, and {f>0}=union_m E_m has measure zero.

Mistakes

  • Adding level sets that are not disjoint.
  • Calling f=0 everywhere rather than a.e.
  • Using the simple-function formula directly for an arbitrary f.
  • Recovery rule: For a signed function, integral f=0 does not imply f=0 a.e.; cancellation is possible, so the nonnegative hypothesis is essential.

Checklist

  • I can define the non-negative integral.
  • I can integrate an indicator and simple function.
  • I can reproduce the level-set proof for zero integral.
