Topology - Most Asked Exam Questions

Consolidated question families from the local MA342 papers, preserving repeated wording, marks, multipart structures, and meaningful variants.

How to Use This Document

Current source scope: the three Tobias Rossmann starred papers: 2023/24, 2024/25, and 2025/26.

Format warning: all three current papers require all four questions, each worth 25 marks, over two hours. Their shared question shape is the strongest prediction evidence. The earlier 2018/19, 2021/22, and 2022/23 papers use different starred examiners and formats, but recurrent legacy families are retained below so they are not lost.

  1. Revise by question family, not just by broad topic headings.
  2. Attempt the listed paper variants before reading the reusable answer frame.
  3. For every definition, memorise the full quantifiers and topology in which it is being stated.
  4. For every proof, identify the requested conclusion first: a separating example, an open cover, a continuous image, or an invariant.

Most Repeated Current Question Families

Each priority consolidates genuinely similar questions, but the paper-list keeps variants that require a different example, theorem, or proof move.

Priority 1 - Topology Definitions, Standard Examples, Subspaces, Bases, Closure, And Interior

Frequency: 3/3 current papers. This is the stable opening-question family.

Appears as:

  • 2023/24 - Q1(a) - define a topology and give two distinct topologies on a finite set. [5 marks]
  • 2023/24 - Q1(b) - define a subspace topology and show the usual subspace topology on Q is not discrete. [5 marks]
  • 2023/24 - Q1(c) - define a basis and give the lower-limit basis. [5 marks]
  • 2023/24 - Q1(d)-(e) - define closure, prove the neighbourhood characterisation, and find the closure of (0,1) in the lower-limit topology. [10 marks]
  • 2024/25 - Q1(a) - repeat topology definition plus two finite topologies. [5 marks]
  • 2024/25 - Q1(b)-(d) - compare cofinite and discrete topologies on Z; show Z is discrete as a subspace of R; give the lower-limit basis and show [0,1) is clopen. [15 marks]
  • 2024/25 - Q1(e) - define neighbourhood and interior, then prove the neighbourhood/interior equivalence. [5 marks]
  • 2025/26 - Q1(a)-(e) - define topology, standard and cofinite topologies, closure, interior and subspace topology; decide whether [0,1] is discrete; prove no accumulation points implies closed. [25 marks]

Consolidated most-asked question:

Let X be a set or topological space. State the exact definition requested, then work with a named topology or subspace: compare two topologies, give a basis, decide whether a subspace is discrete, or prove a closure/interior/accumulation-point claim by neighbourhoods.

Reusable answer: A topology τ on X contains ∅ and X, is closed under arbitrary unions and finite intersections. For subspaces, write opens as A∩U where U is open in the ambient space. For the lower-limit topology use the basis {[a,b):a<b}; for cofinite spaces use ∅ and sets with finite complement. When deciding if a subspace is discrete, test a singleton: an interior point such as 1/2 in [0,1] cannot be relatively open, but Z is discrete in R because each n is isolated by a small interval. For closure use “every neighbourhood meets A”; for accumulation points use “every neighbourhood meets A\{x}”. To prove a set closed from no accumulation points, show every x outside it has an open neighbourhood disjoint from it.

Do not collapse these variants:

  • Q versus Z: Q is not discrete in the usual subspace topology; Z is discrete. The same definition appears, but the witness is different.
  • Standard versus cofinite: an ordinary open interval has infinite complement, so it distinguishes the topologies on R; cofinite and discrete do not coincide on infinite Z.
  • Closure versus interior: closure is a neighbourhood-intersection condition; interior is the largest open subset. An accumulation-point proof removes the point itself.

Priority 2 - Compactness, Hausdorffness, Cofinite And Lower-Limit Examples

Frequency: 3/3 current papers, with both definitions/examples and reusable short theorems.

Appears as:

  • 2023/24 - Q2(a)-(b) - define compactness and decide whether standard R is compact. [9 marks]
  • 2023/24 - Q3(a) - decide whether cofinite R is compact and Hausdorff. [6 marks]
  • 2023/24 - Q3(c) - show a continuous bijection between compact Hausdorff spaces is a homeomorphism. [5 marks]
  • 2024/25 - Q2(a)-(e) - dense subsets of cofinite R; compactness; closed subsets of compact spaces; non-compactness of lower-limit R; and Hausdorff comparisons for cofinite Z and standard R. [25 marks]
  • 2025/26 - Q2(b)-(e) - cofinite density; define compactness and disprove compactness of lower-limit R; define and prove lower-limit Hausdorffness; prove a compact subspace of a Hausdorff space is closed. [22 marks]

Consolidated most-asked question:

For a standard, cofinite, or lower-limit topology, state compactness or Hausdorffness exactly, decide the property using a topology-valid cover or separation, then prove the associated compactness theorem under its stated hypotheses.

Reusable answer: Compact means every open cover has a finite subcover. To disprove compactness of the lower-limit line, use the basic-open cover {[n,n+1):n∈Z}; every finite subfamily is bounded. A cofinite topology on an infinite set is compact but not Hausdorff: every open cover has a finite subcover, while two nonempty cofinite opens must intersect. For lower-limit R, if x<y then [x,y) and [y,y+1) are disjoint basic neighbourhoods, so it is Hausdorff. For a compact A in Hausdorff X, separate x∉A from each a∈A, use compactness to select finitely many neighbourhoods of points of A, and intersect the corresponding finitely many neighbourhoods of x to show X\A is open.

Keep the theorem directions distinct:

  • Closed subset of compact space ⇒ compact (2024/25).
  • Compact subspace of Hausdorff space ⇒ closed (2025/26, also older papers).
  • Continuous bijection compact → Hausdorff ⇒ homeomorphism (2023/24).

Priority 3 - Connectedness, Separations, Continuous Images, And IVT

Frequency: 3/3 current papers. The direct proof and IVT implication repeat, while the example family changes.

Appears as:

  • 2023/24 - Q3(b) - prove a continuous surjective image of a connected space is connected and derive IVT. [8 marks]
  • 2024/25 - Q3(b) - define connectedness and give connected/disconnected examples. [8 marks]
  • 2024/25 - Q4(a) - repeat the continuous-surjection proof and IVT consequence. [6 marks]
  • 2025/26 - Q3(b)-(c) - define connectedness; classify Q with discrete, indiscrete, usual subspace and cofinite topologies, giving separations where required. [13 marks]
  • 2025/26 - Q4(b) - repeat the continuous-surjection proof and IVT consequence. [6 marks]

Consolidated most-asked question:

Define connectedness. Given an explicit topology, either exhibit a separation or rule one out; then prove that a continuous surjection preserves connectedness and explain its Intermediate Value Theorem consequence.

Reusable answer: A separation of X is X=U∪V where U and V are disjoint, nonempty, open subsets of X. A space is connected when no separation exists. If f:X→Y is continuous and onto and Y=U∪V were a separation, f-1(U) and f-1(V) would be a separation of X; continuity gives openness and surjectivity gives nonemptiness and coverage. For IVT, [a,b] is connected, so h([a,b]) is a connected subset of R and hence an interval containing h(a) and h(b).

2025/26 topology-on-Q answers to know:

  • Discrete Q is disconnected: {0} and Q\{0} separate it.
  • Indiscrete Q is connected: it has no nonempty proper open set.
  • Usual subspace Q is disconnected: cut at the irrational √2.
  • Cofinite Q is connected: any two nonempty open sets intersect.

Priority 4 - Homeomorphism Comparisons And Invariants

Frequency: 3/3 current papers. The exact pairs vary, but compactness, connectedness after point removal, and explicit maps recur.

Appears as:

  • 2023/24 - Q2(c) - compare [0,1], (0,1), S1, and R\{0}; justify all six non-homeomorphism comparisons. [12 marks]
  • 2024/25 - Q3(c) - show R and [0,1] are not homeomorphic, R2 and R are not homeomorphic, and R and (0,∞) are homeomorphic. [9 marks]
  • 2025/26 - Q3(d) - show S1 and [0,1] are not homeomorphic, R and [0,1] are not homeomorphic, and R and (0,∞) are homeomorphic. [9 marks]

Consolidated most-asked question:

For each listed pair of spaces, choose the cheapest topological invariant that distinguishes them, or construct an explicit continuous bijection with continuous inverse.

Reusable answer: Homeomorphisms preserve compactness and connectedness, including connectedness after deleting corresponding points. [0,1] is compact and R is not, so they are not homeomorphic. S1 and [0,1] are both compact and connected, so use point removal: delete the preimage of 1/2 under a hypothetical homeomorphism. The punctured circle remains connected, whereas [0,1]\{1/2} is disconnected. For a positive result, f(x)=ex is a homeomorphism R→(0,∞), with inverse ln.

Priority 5 - Product, Initial/Final, And Quotient Topologies

Frequency: 3/3 current papers. The construction changes, so retain the direction of every defining preimage condition.

Appears as:

  • 2023/24 - Q2(d) - write down a base for the product topology on X×Y. [4 marks]
  • 2023/24 - Q4(a)-(b) - define the quotient topology on X/~ and show S1 is a quotient of [0,1]. [9 marks]
  • 2024/25 - Q3(a) - define the initial topology induced by a family of functions. [4 marks]
  • 2024/25 - Q4(b)-(d) - repeat the product basis, quotient topology, and S1 as a quotient of [0,1]. [13 marks]
  • 2025/26 - Q3(a) - define the final topology with respect to a family of functions. [3 marks]
  • 2025/26 - Q4(c)-(d) - explain why S1 is a quotient of [0,1], then decide whether R is a quotient of the Cantor set. [8 marks]

Consolidated most-asked question:

State the construction precisely: basis rectangles for products, pullbacks for initial/final topologies, or the quotient-open-set rule. Then apply it to a quotient map, especially the endpoint identification [0,1]/(0~1), or use a preserved property to rule a quotient out.

Reusable answer: A product basis consists of U×V with U and V open. The initial topology is the coarsest topology making the given maps out of a set continuous; the final topology is the finest topology making the given maps into a set continuous. A quotient topology on X/~ declares W open exactly when q-1(W) is open in X. For S1, use q(t)=e2πit: it is a continuous surjection and identifies exactly 0 and 1. R cannot be a quotient of compact Cantor C because a quotient map would be a continuous surjection C→R, forcing R to be compact.

Direction trap: initial topology concerns maps out of the set and is coarsest; final topology concerns maps into the set and is finest. Quotient topology is a final-topology instance.

Priority 6 - Cantor Set: Definition, Images, Compactness, And Quotients

Frequency: 3/3 current papers, with a different Cantor-set use each year.

Appears as:

  • 2023/24 - Q3(d) - show [0,1] is a continuous image of C, then decide whether C is a continuous image of [0,1]. [6 marks]
  • 2024/25 - Q3(d) - define the Cantor set. [4 marks]
  • 2025/26 - Q4(a) - define C and explain why it is compact Hausdorff. [5 marks]
  • 2025/26 - Q4(d) - decide whether standard R is a quotient of C. [3 marks]

Consolidated most-asked question:

Define the Cantor set, state its compact-Hausdorff properties, and reason carefully about what can be its continuous image or quotient using compactness, connectedness, and an explicit map where supplied.

Reusable answer: Define C by repeatedly removing the open middle third from each remaining closed interval, equivalently as the points of [0,1] with a ternary expansion using only 0 and 2. It is compact because it is closed in compact [0,1], and Hausdorff because it is a subspace of R. Use compactness under continuous images to rule out a continuous surjection or quotient from C onto non-compact R. Do not reverse image claims automatically: [0,1] is a continuous image of C, but connectedness blocks a continuous surjection [0,1]→C because C is disconnected.

Priority 7 - Homotopy, Homotopy Equivalence, And Convex/Star-Shaped Spaces

Frequency: 3/3 current papers. The recurring core is the definition plus a constructive deformation.

Appears as:

  • 2023/24 - Q4(c)-(e) - define homotopy and homotopy equivalence, define simply connected, and show every star-shaped subset of Rn is simply connected. [16 marks]
  • 2024/25 - Q4(e) - define homotopy and show continuous f,g:Rm→Rn are homotopic. [6 marks]
  • 2025/26 - Q4(e) - repeat the homotopy definition and show continuous f,g:Rn→Rn are homotopic. [6 marks]

Consolidated most-asked question:

Define homotopy with its full domain, codomain, continuity and endpoint conditions. Then construct a homotopy by straight-line interpolation in a convex target, or a contraction in a star-shaped space.

Reusable answer: Maps f,g:X→Y are homotopic if a continuous H:X×[0,1]→Y exists with H(x,0)=f(x) and H(x,1)=g(x). For maps into Rn, take H(x,t)=(1−t)f(x)+tg(x). It is continuous and remains in Rn. More generally this works for any convex target. For a star-shaped set with centre x0, H(x,t)=(1−t)x+tx0 contracts the space, provided the entire line segment stays inside it.

Legacy High-Frequency And Distinct Backup Questions

These are deliberately retained. They have strong repetition in the older archive but no direct question-family evidence in the three current Tobias Rossmann papers.

Legacy Priority A - Euler Characteristic, Euler Integral, And Triangulations

Archive frequency: 3/3 older papers (2018/19, 2021/22, 2022/23); absent from 2023/24–2025/26.

  • 2018/19 - Q1-Q3 - triangulate a torus, prove the sphere Euler formula, and calculate an Euler integral from a weighted diagram.
  • 2021/22 - Q1-Q3 - calculate a digital-image Euler characteristic, calculate an Euler integral, and triangulate an identification space from a square.
  • 2022/23 - Q1-Q2 and Q4 - repeat digital-image Euler characteristic, Euler integral, and a detailed valid/invalid torus-triangulation task. [25 marks each]

Define χ=V−E+F or the Euler integral, then calculate it from a cell complex/weighted diagram; alternatively validate, construct, and count a triangulation before computing χ.

Backup answer frame: State precisely what is being counted and what assumptions permit the formula. For a triangulation, list vertices, edges and faces without double-counting and check that simplices meet correctly. For a weighted Euler integral, identify the level sets or use the inclusion-exclusion relation supplied in the question, showing each contribution before deriving the requested number of regions.

Legacy Priority B - Brouwer, FTA, Frobenius-Perron, And Nash Equilibrium

Archive frequency: 3/3 older papers as alternative long questions; absent from 2023/24–2025/26.

  • 2018/19 - Q9-Q10 - Brouwer fixed point and positive eigenvalues, FTA via winding number, and Nash equilibrium.
  • 2021/22 - Q8-Q10 - Brouwer/positive eigenvalue, FTA, Brouwer via Euler characteristic, a surjection onto a triangle, and Nash equilibrium.
  • 2022/23 - Q9-Q10 - Brouwer/Frobenius-Perron, FTA, and a mixed Nash-equilibrium game calculation. [25 marks each]

State the named theorem with all hypotheses, map the supplied algebraic or game-theoretic data to it, and derive exactly the requested consequence.

Backup answer frame: For Brouwer, verify a continuous self-map of a compact convex set such as a ball/simplex. For FTA questions, state exactly which homotopy/winding result has been granted before using it. For Nash questions, define strategies, payoffs and best responses; distinguish pure from mixed strategies and justify the equilibrium rather than naming one.

Other Distinct Older Question Shapes

  • Topology-axiom validation and components: test proposed open-set families, exhibit a failed axiom if necessary, and find connected components if valid. Appears in 2018/19 Q4, 2021/22 Q4, and 2022/23 Q3.
  • Cofinite-space continuity: decide connectedness/compactness and give continuous or non-continuous self-maps of a cofinite space. Appears in 2018/19 Q5 and 2021/22 Q5; it complements the current cofinite compactness/Hausdorff family.
  • Homotopy-equivalence variants: show S1 is homotopy equivalent to C\{0}, or classify plane letters by homeomorphism/homotopy type. Appears in 2018/19 Q8, 2021/22 Q7, and 2022/23 Q8.
  • Graph/dendrogram application: construct threshold graphs and a dendrogram from a distance table. Appears only in 2022/23 Q6.
  • Older homeomorphism pair: decide whether Z and Q are homeomorphic in their usual subspace topologies. Appears only in 2021/22 Q6.

Recommended Study Order

  1. Topology definitions, standard/cofinite/lower-limit examples, subspaces, closure/interior, and accumulation points.
  2. Compactness and Hausdorffness: lower-limit/cofinite examples and the two compactness theorems.
  3. Connectedness: separations, the four topologies on Q, continuous images, and IVT.
  4. Homeomorphism comparisons, using compactness or punctured-space connectedness before attempting complicated invariants.
  5. Product, initial/final and quotient constructions, especially S1 from [0,1].
  6. Cantor-set definitions, images, compactness and quotient obstructions.
  7. Homotopy definitions and straight-line constructions in Rn.
  8. Only then revise Euler/triangulation and Brouwer/FTA/Nash legacy material, unless current lectures explicitly return to it.

For timed practice, start with 2025/26, then 2024/25 and 2023/24. Use the older papers to preserve the legacy families above, not to override the stable current exam format.
