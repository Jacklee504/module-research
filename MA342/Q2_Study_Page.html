2. Dense subsets Asked 2/3 years

Explanation: A subset \(A\) is dense in \(X\) when every non-empty open set in \(X\) contains at least one point of \(A\).
Exam definition: A subset \(A\subseteq X\) is dense in \(X\) if
\[ \overline A=X. \]

Equivalently, every non-empty open subset of \(X\) meets \(A\).

Example of a proper dense subset of \(\mathbb Z\) with the cofinite topology

Let \[ A=2\mathbb Z, \] the set of even integers. The set \(A\) is not equal to \(\mathbb Z\), but it is infinite. Every non-empty open set in the cofinite topology has finite complement. Therefore it cannot avoid the infinite set \(A\). Hence every non-empty open set meets \(A\), so \(A\) is dense in \(\mathbb Z\).

Show that every infinite subset of \(\mathbb R\) is dense in the cofinite topology

Worked answer

Let \(A\subseteq\mathbb R\) be infinite.

Let \(U\) be a non-empty open set in the cofinite topology. Then \(\mathbb R\setminus U\) is finite.

If \(U\cap A=\varnothing\), then \(A\subseteq\mathbb R\setminus U\). This would place the infinite set \(A\) inside a finite set, which is impossible.

Therefore every non-empty open set \(U\) meets \(A\). Hence \(A\) is dense in \(\mathbb R\).

3. Compact topological spaces Asked 3/3 years

Explanation: A space is compact if, whenever open sets cover the whole space, finitely many of those open sets are already enough to cover it.
Exam definition: A topological space \(X\) is compact if every open cover of \(X\) has a finite subcover.
If \[ X\subseteq\bigcup_{\alpha\in I}U_\alpha, \] where every \(U_\alpha\) is open, then there exist \(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n\in I\) such that \[ X\subseteq U_{\alpha_1}\cup\cdots\cup U_{\alpha_n}. \]

Meaning of the terms

  • An open cover is a collection of open sets whose union contains all of \(X\).
  • A finite subcover is a finite selection of those open sets that still covers all of \(X\).

4. Closed subsets of compact spaces are compact

Key idea: Add the open complement of the closed subset to its open cover. This gives an open cover of the whole compact space.
Exam-ready proof

Let \(X\) be compact and let \(A\subseteq X\) be closed.

Suppose \(\{U_\alpha\}_{\alpha\in I}\) is an open cover of \(A\).

Since \(A\) is closed, \(X\setminus A\) is open. Therefore

\[ \{U_\alpha:\alpha\in I\} \cup \{X\setminus A\} \]

is an open cover of \(X\).

Since \(X\) is compact, this cover has a finite subcover. Removing \(X\setminus A\), if it was selected, leaves finitely many of the sets \(U_\alpha\) that cover \(A\).

Therefore \(A\) is compact.

5. Is \(\mathbb R\) compact?

Standard topology

Answer: The real line \(\mathbb R\) with the standard topology is not compact.
Justification using an open cover

Consider the open cover

\[ \mathcal U = \{(-n,n):n\in\mathbb N\}. \]

These intervals cover \(\mathbb R\), since every real number lies in \((-n,n)\) for some sufficiently large \(n\).

However, any finite selection has a largest value \(N\), and its union is contained in \((-N,N)\). This does not cover all of \(\mathbb R\).

Therefore the cover has no finite subcover, so \(\mathbb R\) is not compact.

Lower limit topology

Answer: The real line with the lower limit topology is not compact.
Justification

Consider the collection

\[ \mathcal U = \{[-n,n):n\in\mathbb N\}. \]

Each interval \([-n,n)\) is open in the lower limit topology, and the collection covers \(\mathbb R\).

Any finite selection has a largest value \(N\), so its union is contained in \([-N,N)\), which does not cover all of \(\mathbb R\).

Therefore there is no finite subcover, so the lower limit real line is not compact.

7. Compactness and Hausdorffness of cofinite spaces

Is \(\mathbb Z\) with the cofinite topology Hausdorff?

Answer: No. The cofinite topology on the infinite set \(\mathbb Z\) is not Hausdorff.
Justification

Suppose \(U\) and \(V\) are non-empty open sets in the cofinite topology.

Their complements \(\mathbb Z\setminus U\) and \(\mathbb Z\setminus V\) are finite.

If \(U\cap V=\varnothing\), then

\[ \mathbb Z = (\mathbb Z\setminus U) \cup (\mathbb Z\setminus V). \]

The right-hand side is finite, which would imply that \(\mathbb Z\) is finite. This is impossible.

Therefore any two non-empty open sets intersect. Distinct points cannot have disjoint open neighbourhoods, so the space is not Hausdorff.

Is \(\mathbb R\) with the cofinite topology compact?

Answer: Yes. Every set with the cofinite topology is compact.
Justification

Let \(\{U_\alpha\}_{\alpha\in I}\) be an open cover of \(\mathbb R\).

Choose one non-empty member \(U_{\alpha_0}\). Since it is open in the cofinite topology,

\[ \mathbb R\setminus U_{\alpha_0} \]

is finite.

For each remaining point in this finite complement, choose one set from the cover containing that point.

Together with \(U_{\alpha_0}\), these finitely many sets cover \(\mathbb R\). Hence the space is compact.

8. Compact subsets of Hausdorff spaces are closed

Key idea: Separate a point outside the compact set from each point inside it. Compactness reduces the resulting open cover to finitely many sets.
Exam-ready proof

Let \(X\) be Hausdorff and let \(A\subseteq X\) be compact. We show that \(X\setminus A\) is open.

Let \(x\in X\setminus A\). For every \(a\in A\), the Hausdorff property gives disjoint open sets \(U_a\) and \(V_a\) such that

\[ x\in U_a, \qquad a\in V_a. \]

The collection \(\{V_a:a\in A\}\) is an open cover of \(A\). Since \(A\) is compact, there exist \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\in A\) such that

\[ A \subseteq V_{a_1}\cup\cdots\cup V_{a_n}. \]

Define

\[ U = U_{a_1}\cap\cdots\cap U_{a_n}. \]

Then \(U\) is open and contains \(x\). Also, \(U\) is disjoint from every \(V_{a_i}\), so \(U\cap A=\varnothing\).

Hence \(U\subseteq X\setminus A\). Every point of \(X\setminus A\) therefore has an open neighbourhood contained in \(X\setminus A\).

Thus \(X\setminus A\) is open, so \(A\) is closed.

9. Showing spaces are not homeomorphic

Explanation: Homeomorphic spaces must share topological properties such as compactness, connectedness and the number of connected components.
The 2023/24 paper asked for comparisons among \([0,1]\), \((0,1)\), \(S^1\), and \(\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}\).

\([0,1]\) and \((0,1)\)

They are not homeomorphic because \([0,1]\) is compact, while \((0,1)\) is not compact.

\([0,1]\) and \(S^1\)

They are not homeomorphic. Removing an interior point from \([0,1]\) disconnects it, while removing any one point from \(S^1\) leaves a connected space homeomorphic to an open interval.

\([0,1]\) and \(\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}\)

They are not homeomorphic because \([0,1]\) is connected, while \(\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}\) is disconnected.

\((0,1)\) and \(S^1\)

They are not homeomorphic because \(S^1\) is compact, while \((0,1)\) is not compact.

\((0,1)\) and \(\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}\)

They are not homeomorphic because \((0,1)\) is connected, while \(\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}\) is disconnected.

\(S^1\) and \(\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}\)

They are not homeomorphic because \(S^1\) is connected and compact, while \(\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}\) is disconnected and non-compact.

10. Base for the product topology

Explanation: Basic open sets in \(X\times Y\) are products of open sets from \(X\) and \(Y\).
Exam answer: If \(X\) and \(Y\) are topological spaces, a base for the product topology on \(X\times Y\) is
\[ \mathcal B = \left\{ U\times V: U\text{ is open in }X,\; V\text{ is open in }Y \right\}. \]
